\begin{abstract} 
\noindent
% 84 words
\noindent Governments increasingly regulate sin goods through procedural frictions rather than prices or quantities. Using bank transaction data, we evaluate Australia's 2024 ban on credit cards for online wagering. As affected gamblers could gamble the same amount through a transaction account, the ban imposed a friction rather than a constraint. Participation fell 15 percentage points, but the response was self-selecting: those who stopped were low-stakes bettors, drawn from a population already positively selected on financial capacity, while heavier gamblers switched routes and continued. Unlike ordeals in transfer programs, payment frictions may screen out the users regulators worry about least.
\\\\

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} Sports betting, gambling, payment frictions, ordeals, targeting, household finance.

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Governments increasingly regulate sin goods by restricting how it can be financed rather than affecting prices and quantities. Payment restrictions are politically useful in that they leave the good legal and accessible to the majority of the population, while aiming to screen out the transactions thought most likely to generate harm. The logic is particularly intuitive for gambling on credit, as it allows someone to keep betting after liquid funds are exhausted, which convert losses into high-interest debt. This rationale motivated Australia's 2024 ban on credit cards for online wagering and similar rules in Great Britain and Ireland \citep{Rowland_credit_cards_ban_2024,GamblingCommission2020_CreditCardBan,IrelandGamblingRegulationAct2024}. Announcing the Australian ban, the responsible minister argued that ``people should not be betting with money they don't have'' \citep{Rowland_credit_cards_ban_2024}. %Yet the parliamentary committee acknowledged that evidence linking online gambling harm to credit-card use was limited \citep[p.~19]{RegulationUseFinancialServicesOnlineGamblingAus}.

The obvious problem with this theory is fungibility. Where gambling via credit is banned, a customer could transfer card funds to a transaction account and bet from there, or simply just move other consumption around between payment mechanisms. %Both transactions were treated as cash advances, so the workaround preserved access to the same credit line at approximately the same pecuniary cost. The policy closed a route without necessarily removing the resource behind it, letting us distinguish a binding liquidity constraint from the behavioural value of a payment method. 
Figure \ref{fig:rawdata} shows this mechanism occuring around Australia's credit card ban. For those affected, gambling on credit cards mechanically drops to zero - consistent with the ban being succesfully implemented - but transaction-account wagering rises to offset this. However, the increase only offsets part of the card card decline. This is due to the additional affect of these policies: that they create a behavioural friction in requiring gamblers to substitute between modes.

Our contribution is about who adjusts. The response is concentrated at the extensive margin: participation falls by 10--15 percentage points. We find a variety of pieces of evidence that this is due to frictions that the policy introduced Stopping is steeply graded by attachment and payment setup and flat in financial position. Stopping, meaning no observed wagering in the six post-ban weeks, falls from 66 per cent in the lowest quartile of pre-ban wagering to 15 per cent in the highest. Gamblers who had already wagered from both a card and a transaction account are 44 percentage points less likely to stop than those reliant on the card alone, a single pre-determined indicator that raises the $R^2$ from 0.15 to 0.30. Liquidity-constraint status, revolving status, balances, and card debt add essentially nothing.

Australia offers an ideal setting Sports betting has been legal since 1983 and online wagering federally regulated since 2001, and Australians lose more per capita to gambling than any other population \citep{Peake2025_biggest_losers}. Unlike newly legalised markets, Australia is a mature wagering market, so the response we measure reflects established consumers adjusting to a restriction rather than consumers and firms adjusting to legalisation.



The gradient has a direct implication. Because the people who stop are disproportionately those who wagered least, their share of pre-ban wagering is smaller than their share of affected gamblers. Observed stoppers therefore account for far more of the affected headcount than of pre-ban wagering dollars. This conclusion requires no assumption about harm; it follows from the observed selection into stopping.

This inverts the logic of ordeals. In transfer programs, an application cost screens \emph{in} the high-need claimants precisely because willingness to bear it rises with need \citep{NicholsZeckhauser1982,AlatasEtAl2016_SelfTargeting,DeshpandeLi2019_ScreenedOut,ShepardWagner2025_Ordeals}. Here willingness to bear the ordeal rises with attachment, and the planner wants the attached to select \emph{out}. Selection runs the same way in both cases. What reverses is where the planner wants the targeted type to end up. Fixed payment hurdles and ad valorem taxes therefore load costs onto different parts of the market. A tax's mechanical burden rises with wagering expenditure; a fixed hurdle does not. The former scales with dollars, while the latter creates an extensive-margin threshold that bites hardest at the casual edge.

The second selection margin operates before the policy does. Exposure requires holding a credit card, a product screened on financial standing. Card gamblers in our data have higher incomes and larger liquid balances than comparison gamblers and are no more likely to revolve debt or to run a transaction balance below \$100, though within the affected group these markers of stress rise with wagering intensity. We have no clinical measure of problem gambling, and group averages conceal vulnerable individuals, so none of this shows that affected gamblers were unharmed. It does show that exposure was poorly aligned with vulnerability defined by low liquidity. The policy reached people positively selected on observed financial capacity rather than those excluded from a screened credit product.

Cash-advance fees make the value of the route concrete. Before the ban, most affected gamblers held enough liquid funds to wager from a transaction account without a fee, yet used the card and incurred a fee of roughly three per cent. This may reveal a valuable convenience or standing preference for the card route, or it may reflect inattention to the fee. After the ban, average cash-advance fees fall by \$3--\$4 per fortnight and their incidence falls by 25--30 percentage points. The decline does not distinguish a friction from a binding constraint, but it shows that customers did not systematically preserve card borrowing through the transfer workaround. Had they done so, the cash-advance fee would have followed them.

Three literatures frame the exercise. Behavioral public finance studies instruments intended to correct self-control problems in temptation markets \citep{GruberKoszegi2001_Addiction,ODonoghueRabin2006_OptimalSinTaxes,AllcottLockwoodTaubinsky2019,SchmackerSmed2023_SinTaxes}, and work on frictions and sludge shows that small procedural costs move behaviour \citep{Thaler2018_Sludge}; we add the payment route as an instrument and show that its selectivity works against the targeting objective. Household finance shows that restricting expensive credit changes distress and induces substitution into other borrowing channels \citep{Melzer2011_CreditAccess,Zinman2010_RestrictingCredit,Morse2011_PaydayLenders}; our setting isolates a different margin, because credit remains available, separating a binding resource constraint from the behavioral value of a route. Related evidence shows that payment instruments themselves affect spending through salience, timing, and mental accounts \citep{Feinberg1986_CreditCardStimuli,PrelecSimester2001_CreditCardEffect,Soman2001_PaymentMechanism,Jorring2023_PaymentChoice,GelmanRoussanov2024_MentalAccounts}. On gambling specifically, problem gambling is associated with financial stress, poorer mental health, crime, and harm to families \citep{Langham2016_gambling_harm_taxonomy,Adolphe2019_crime_review,Dowling2014_family_violence}, and recent work finds that expanded access to sports betting reduces saving and worsens credit outcomes \citep{BakerEtAl2024_GamblingAwayStability,HollenbeckEtAl2025_FinancialConsequences}, while transaction-data studies document strong associations between gambling intensity and financial difficulty \citep{MuggletonEtAl2021_BigFinancialData,MarionneauEtAl2023_IndebtedBankTransactions,ZendleNewall2024_OpenBankingGambling}. Where that literature studies market expansion, we study the reverse experiment: an attempted contraction among established consumers.

\section{Setting and framework}\label{sec:framework}

\subsection{The ban closed a route without closing a credit line}

Australia prohibited credit cards for in-person gambling in 2006, but online wagering remained exempt. The Federal Government announced an online ban on 7 December 2023 and implemented it on 11 June 2024. Wagering firms had to block deposits originating from credit cards, including deposits routed through digital wallets; online lotteries were exempt \citep{RegulationUseFinancialServicesOnlineGamblingAus,Rowland_credit_cards_ban_2024}.

Credit-card gambling was already expensive. Banks classified a wagering deposit as a cash advance, commonly charging about three per cent or a small minimum fee and accruing interest immediately \citep{AustralianBankingAssociation2020UseCreditCardsGambling}. Funding the same deposit from money already in a transaction account avoided those charges entirely. Some gamblers nevertheless chose the card and paid an explicit premium to do so.

The ban left two alternatives. A customer could fund wagering from liquid balances and avoid the fee, or preserve access to card credit by transferring funds to a transaction account and depositing from there. Because that transfer was also a cash advance, the second option preserved both the credit line and approximately the same pecuniary cost. What changed was the route. The required adjustment differed across people in a way that maps directly to our data: some affected gamblers held no debit card at all; some held one but had not registered it with the operator; and some had both routes already loaded and simply preferred the card.\footnote{The cost need not be pecuniary. It can reflect an established routine, use of the card as a separate mental account, its security features, or a wish to keep wagering out of an account observed by a partner. Inattention to the pre-existing cash-advance fee may also have made the card route appear cheaper than it was. These are unobserved and none is necessarily a marker of gambling harm.}

Great Britain provides the closest precedent. Its 2020 ban was explicitly intended to add friction to gambling with borrowed money while recognising that indirect uses of credit would remain \citep{GamblingCommission2021_Evaluation}. A later mixed-method evaluation found the ban was implemented successfully but that friction did not uniformly change gambling or borrowing; people reporting no or low gambling problems were more likely than those reporting moderate or high problems to report reduced use of credit \citep{NatCen2024_CreditCardBan}. That is the survey analogue of what we measure in linked accounts. Australian evidence also suggests that mild convenience frictions move gambling: a ban on smoking in gaming areas of clubs and hotels reduced poker-machine expenditure across several states \citep{ProductivityCommission2010_AustraliasGamblingIndustries}.

\subsection{A friction, not a constraint}\label{sec:model}

Because the workaround preserved access to the credit line at approximately the same pecuniary cost, the ban changed what an affected gambler had to do to spend rather than necessarily changing how much they could spend. Let $V_i$ denote the value of continuing to wager over the post-policy horizon, net of ordinary monetary costs, and let $k_i \geq 0$ denote the non-pecuniary cost of reorganising payment. An exposed gambler continues through a permitted route when
\begin{equation}\label{eq:continue}
    \mathrm{Continue}_i = \mathbf{1}\{V_i \geq k_i\}.
\end{equation}

Because the hurdle is paid to continue at all, it operates through participation and gives no reason for deposits among continuers to fall. Prior wagering measures attachment to the activity. Prior use of both a card and a transaction account identifies gamblers for whom the permitted route was already set up, removing much of the setup component of the hurdle. These mappings are specified before the results; the associations below test whether selection follows them. Neither $V_i$ nor $k_i$ is observed, so we do not estimate the size of the hurdle.

This distinguishes a hurdle from a price instrument. A gambler wagering $g_i$ bears $k_i$ under the hurdle and $\tau g_i$ under an ad valorem tax at rate $\tau$: the hurdle's mechanical burden is invariant to how much a person wagers, while the tax's is proportional to it. The hurdle's relative importance is therefore greatest for low-intensity users.

The empirical counterpart is a simple composition identity. Let $S_i$ indicate no observed wagering during the six post-ban weeks and let $q_i$ be pre-ban wagering deposits. The share of pre-ban wagering attributable to observed stoppers is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:composition}
    \frac{\mathbb{E}[q_iS_i]}{\mathbb{E}[q_i]}
    = \mathbb{E}[S_i] + \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(q_i,S_i)}{\mathbb{E}[q_i]}.
\end{equation}
Stopping declines steeply with prior wagering in our data, so $\operatorname{Cov}(q_i,S_i)<0$: observed stoppers account for a smaller share of pre-ban wagering than their share of affected gamblers. This is a descriptive composition result, not a causal estimate of wagering eliminated by the policy. It gives the headcount-versus-dollars distinction a direct empirical object.

Two forms of selection follow. Exposure requires both access to a screened credit product and pre-ban use of that product for wagering. Card access rises with financial capacity, so the first stage tilts exposure toward stronger financial standing. The pre-policy data confirm that this selection survives the choice to use the card for wagering.

The competing liquidity mechanism is that the ban reduced purchasing power in practice, perhaps because users lacked liquid funds, could not access the transfer workaround, or did not understand it. A broadly binding constraint should be most visible among users dependent on card credit or low liquidity and may compress deposits among those who continue. If affected users could reach the workaround, it also implies rerouting, with card borrowing moving to the transaction account and cash-advance fees persisting; a constrained user who could not reach it would stop and cease paying fees. A payment friction instead predicts substitution toward transaction accounts, exit at the extensive margin, little necessary change in deposits among continuers, and substantially less exit among people already familiar with the permitted route. No single pattern identifies the mechanism, so we assess them jointly.

The distinction between a one-time setup cost and a standing preference matters for what the policy does over time. A setup cost decays as persistent gamblers reorganise payment; a recurring convenience loss endures. Six weeks cannot separate them.

\section{Data and empirical design}\label{sec:design}

\subsection{Data and measurement}

We use consented, aggregated, and de-identified bank transaction data collected by a third-party provider during consumer-finance applications. The source is selected: users are applying for financial products and skew young and liquidity constrained relative to the Australian population. We therefore interpret our estimates as effects for observed card gamblers, not for all gamblers or all people experiencing gambling harm. The card share of wagering deposits in our sample is below public industry figures.\footnote{Responsible Wagering Australia reported that approximately 20 per cent of deposits were transacted by card, while TAB reported 14 per cent \citep{AustralianBankingAssociation2020UseCreditCardsGambling,RegulationUseFinancialServicesOnlineGamblingAus}.}

Each person contributes a backward-looking history, typically about 90 days, covering transactions and balances across linked transaction, savings, and credit-card accounts. The provider categorises and aggregates transactions and removes personally identifiable information. Linking account types within a person is what makes the design possible: we can attribute each wagering deposit to its funding source and observe money moving between accounts when the card route closes.

We identify deposits into sports-betting accounts from merchant codes verified against major Australian operators. Deposits measure transfers to operators, not net losses: we do not observe bets funded from a balance already held with the operator, winnings left there, or cash gambling. PayPal-routed transactions are attributed to the linked funding account when visible; because a pre-funded wallet could escape this classification, we test separately for changes in PayPal deposits and find none.

Income is regular wage or income-support inflows and expenditure covers services and non-durables. We classify a person as liquidity constrained if their transaction balance falls below \$100 at any point before the policy, and as a revolver if card debt never falls below \$100. We exclude apparent cardholders whose card account is not linked, since they would otherwise be misclassified as non-cardholders.

\subsection{Event-study design}

We build balanced fortnightly panels spanning 4 April to 14 August in 2023 and 2024. Individuals must be observed throughout the six weeks either side of the policy date, have at least one monthly income inflow and five monthly expenditure outflows, and wager before 11 June. Fortnightly aggregation smooths pay and betting cycles; the roughly 90-day account histories limit the post-policy horizon.

The 2024 treatment group consists of people who funded sports wagering from a credit card before the ban. We use two comparison groups. Transaction-account gamblers in 2024 absorb the common sporting calendar, but they are selected on the very margin under study: they chose not to fund wagering with a card. Credit-card gamblers observed over the same dates in 2023 are selected on the same payment behaviour as the treated group, but face a different sporting calendar: 2023 included the Ashes and the FIFA Women's World Cup, 2024 the T20 World Cup and the Euros. Our preferred specification is therefore the unmatched triple difference across year and card status, which removes the 2023 difference between card and transaction-account gamblers and the across-year difference among transaction-account gamblers. It requires only that the change in the card/transaction gap across years be zero absent the policy, rather than that the groups be comparable in levels. Our event study is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:eventstudy}
    y_{it}=\alpha_i+\gamma_t+\sum_{\tau\ne-1}\beta_{\tau}
    \left(\mathrm{Treat}_i\times\mathbf{1}\{t=\tau\}\right)+\varepsilon_{it},
\end{equation}
where $\alpha_i$ and $\gamma_t$ are individual and period fixed effects and the omitted period is the fortnight before implementation. Outcomes include wagering deposits, participation, funding source, balances, other expenditure, and cash-advance fees. We report the two simple differences and a matched specification alongside.

Two features of the timing deserve note. First, the ban was announced on 7 December 2023 and implemented six months later, so anticipatory adjustment may already be embedded in our 2024 pre-period and attenuate estimates at implementation. Second, sample entry is driven by the timing of consumer-finance applications. We apply identical balanced-window requirements across treatment and comparison groups, but the resulting estimates remain local to people whose account histories span the policy window.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{The rule reached gamblers with stronger finances}\label{sec:composition}

The first selection margin is visible before the policy operates. Direct card use was uncommon and declining. Conditional on holding a card, the share of sports-wagering deposits funded from that card fell from about 17 per cent in late 2019 to 8--9 per cent before the ban. A different denominator gives the second fact: by early 2024, only about 2 per cent of all cardholders recorded any card-funded sports wagering (Appendix Figure \ref{fig:cardtime}). The median affected account wagered about \$14 per week.

Those who used the route are not the financially marginal. Card gamblers wager more and carry more outstanding card debt than comparison groups (Appendix Figure \ref{fig:debt}), but they also have higher incomes and larger liquid balances, and they are no more likely to revolve debt or to let a transaction balance fall below \$100 (Appendix Figure \ref{fig:incidence}). Even in the top quartile of their own wagering distribution, most deposits come from transaction accounts: the right tail is not primarily card-financed (Appendix Figure \ref{fig:skew}). The policy reached gamblers who had passed a private credit screen and who, in these data, were positively selected on observed financial capacity.

\subsection{Substitution is incomplete and the response is extensive-margin}

Figure \ref{fig:effects} reports the event studies. Card-funded wagering falls by about \$100 per fortnight and the probability of using a card falls mechanically to zero. Transaction-account wagering rises by about \$50 per fortnight and the probability of funding wagering from a transaction account rises by roughly 20--25 percentage points. Substitution is substantial but incomplete: the new route replaces roughly half the card decline.

The two margins are estimated very differently. The probability of any wagering falls by 10--15 percentage points, with flat pre-periods and estimates stable across the within-year, across-year, triple-difference, and matched specifications. The implied fall in total deposits is around \$50 per fortnight, but that estimate is imprecise and pre-period coefficients on the dollar outcome are of comparable magnitude to post-period ones. This is what the data should produce. The deposit distribution is extremely right-skewed, with mean fortnightly wagering ranging from roughly \$10 in the lowest pre-policy quartile to approximately \$1,000 in the highest (Appendix Figure \ref{fig:skew}). A handful of heavy gamblers dominates the dollar variance while contributing one observation each to participation. We treat participation as the estimated effect and the dollar figure as an implied magnitude.

We find no evidence of displacement into the observed proxies for other routes. There is no detectable increase in cash withdrawals or deposits into PayPal balances, and no offsetting increase when exempt online lotteries are added to the outcome. Estimates using four-, six-, and eight-week balanced windows are similar. We cannot observe cash gambling or balances already held with an operator.

Figure \ref{fig:margins} decomposes the response. Participation among treated gamblers falls from about 75 per cent to 48 per cent in the fortnight of implementation, while comparison groups decline to around 62 per cent on the common sporting calendar. Deposits conditional on wagering show no compression. Because the composition of participants changes after the policy, this is not an unchanged within-person intensive margin. The narrower claim is enough: the aggregate reduction comes from fewer people depositing rather than from smaller deposits, a pattern consistent with a fixed payment hurdle.

\subsection{The friction screened out the casual edge of the market}\label{sec:mechanisms}

We call people with no observed wagering in the six post-policy weeks \emph{stoppers}; they are 38 per cent of the treated group. Because 60 per cent of eventual stoppers wagered in a typical pre-policy fortnight, six consecutive weeks without a deposit is a marked change, though not necessarily permanent exit.

Table \ref{tab:stoppers} relates stopping to characteristics measured before the ban. Stopping falls monotonically across quartiles of pre-ban wagering, from 66 per cent in the lowest to 15 per cent in the highest. Gamblers who had already wagered from both a card and a transaction account are 44 percentage points less likely to stop, and that single indicator raises the $R^2$ from 0.15 to 0.30. Liquidity-constraint status, revolving status, balances, and card debt add nothing. Stopper status is a post-policy outcome, so these are associations rather than treatment effects; they track the two margins in equation \eqref{eq:continue}.

The ``both accounts'' coefficient is the strongest mechanism-consistent pattern in the table. If the ban imposed a setup cost, people who had already used the alternative route should be more likely to continue, and they are by a wide margin. No measure of liquidity or debt comes close. One feature of the measure does warrant a check. Dual-route use records whether a gambler ever funded wagering from both accounts before the ban, so those who deposited more often had more opportunities to qualify. Part of the association, and part of the attenuation in the quartile coefficients, may therefore reflect deposit frequency rather than payment setup; defining dual-route use as a share of pre-ban deposits would separate them.

The intensity gradient also means that stoppers represent a smaller share of pre-ban wagering than of affected gamblers, exactly as equation \eqref{eq:composition} shows. This is composition, not a causal estimate of dollars eliminated. Across the same quartiles, liquidity-constraint incidence and revolving rise (Appendix Figure \ref{fig:skew}), so the group least likely to stop is also the most financially stressed. The unconditional gradient and the conditional null describe different things: distress rises with gambling intensity, and adds no predictive power once prior wagering and payment-route experience are included.

\subsection{Cash-advance fees show that card credit was abandoned, not rerouted}

Fees fall by roughly \$3--\$4 per fortnight and the probability of incurring one falls by 25--30 percentage points (Figure \ref{fig:fees}). The decline does not by itself distinguish a friction from a binding constraint, since both close the card channel. It is, however, evidence against systematic use of the workaround: had customers generally preserved card borrowing by transferring through a transaction account, the cash-advance fee would have followed them.

The constraint account can reconcile the fee collapse only by adding ignorance of, or inability to use, the workaround. Under either version, the element that binds is informational or procedural rather than the removal of purchasing power: the credit line itself remained available.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \caption{Cash-advance fees collapse after the ban}
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{Figures/fB18_cash_advance_fees.pdf}
    \label{fig:fees}
\end{figure}

The fee also bounds the value of the route, though loosely. Conditional on paying, affected gamblers incurred on the order of \$10 per fortnight to use the card when a fee-free transaction-account route was often available. Either the convenience was worth hundreds of dollars a year to some users, or those users were substantially inattentive to a recurring fee. Both explanations make payment routes behaviourally meaningful, but they have opposite welfare implications. If the convenience was genuinely worth the fee, removing it is a cost to users; if the fee went unnoticed, the ban delivered a saving. We cannot separate these explanations, and we do not interpret the fee as an estimate of $k_i$.

We detect little short-run change in non-gambling expenditure or transaction-account balances. Card debt declines gradually by about \$500 on average, concentrated in the right tail. That is what a binding constraint operating in the tail would produce, and the estimate is too imprecise to reject one: a \$50 fortnightly effect is hard to detect against normal balance volatility over six weeks. The rest of the pattern runs the other way. Deposits among continuers show no compression, liquidity plays no conditional role, and dual-route users were 44 points less likely to stop.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

Australia's ban closed a route, not a resource, but routes mattered. Customers could preserve the same credit line at approximately the same cost by adding a transfer. Substitution was nevertheless incomplete, and participation fell by 10--15 percentage points. A payment friction changed consumption without tightening the budget set.

The reach of the instrument matters as much as its behavioural effect, and here the two work against each other. Exposure required a credit card, so the affected population was positively selected on observed financial capacity. Continuation required willingness to overcome the payment hurdle, and stopping was least common among heavier and dual-route gamblers. A payment restriction therefore operates as an ordeal, but the self-selection that makes ordeals useful for allocating transfers can work against the objective of deterring intensive consumption. Because observed stoppers were disproportionately casual gamblers, the fall in participation need not imply a comparable fall in wagering.

Three limits bound this. Our window is six weeks, so a setup cost and a standing preference cannot be told apart, and we cannot separate a friction from a mistaken belief that the ban had closed off card credit entirely. Our design conditions on pre-ban card wagering, so it cannot test whether the same hurdle deters entry, which is where a friction is most likely to earn its keep. And harm is outside our data: the composition result stands on its own, but reading welfare from it requires knowing how harm varies with attachment.

Financial fungibility did not imply behavioural fungibility. A restriction aimed at a payment instrument selected both who was exposed and who was willing to continue, and on both margins it selected against the users it was meant to reach. Reaching them requires instruments that bind harder as use intensifies, not higher hurdles at the door.
